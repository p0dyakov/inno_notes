Epsilon-Delta Definition of a Limit

Definition A function \(f(x)\) is said to have limit \(L\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there exists a \(\delta > 0\) such that \[0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon.\]

Alternating Series Test (Leibniz Test)

Consider an alternating series of the form \[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n+1} a_n\]

The series converges if the following conditions hold: * a) \(a_n > 0\) for all \(n\); * b) \(a_n\) is monotonically decreasing: \(a_{n+1} \le a_n\); * c) \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0\).

If these conditions are satisfied, then the series converges.

Absolute and Conditional Convergence

Absolute Convergence The series \(\sum a_n\) is said to be absolutely convergent if \(\sum |a_n|\) converges.

Conditional Convergence If \(\sum a_n\) is convergent, but not absolutely convergent, then we say that it is conditionally convergent or that it converges conditionally.

Power Series

Definition A series of the form \[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n(x - c)^n = a_0 + a_1(x - c) + a_2(x - c)^2 + a_3(x - c)^3 + \dots\] is called a power series in powers of \(x - c\) or a power series about \(c\). The constants \(a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots\) are called the coefficients of the power series. The point \(c\) is called the center of convergence.

Slide 10

For any power series \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n(x - c)^n\) one of the following alternatives must hold:

  • i) The series may converge only at \(x = c\),
  • ii) The series may converge at every real number \(x\), or
  • iii) There may exist a positive real number \(R\) such that the series converges at every \(x\) satisfying \(|x - c| < R\) and diverges at every \(x\) satisfying \(|x - c| > R\). In this case the series may or may not converge at either of the two endpoints \(x = c - R\) and \(x = c + R\).

In each of these cases the convergence is absolute except possibly at the endpoints \(x = c - R\) and \(x = c + R\) in case (iii).

Slide 11

Interval of Convergence

The set of values \(x\) for which a power series converges is an interval centered at \(x = c\). This interval is called the interval of convergence of the power series. It must have one of the following forms:

  • i) The isolated point \(x = c\),
  • ii) The entire line \((-\infty, \infty)\),
  • iii) A finite interval centered at \(c\): \([c - R, c + R]\), or \([c - R, c + R)\), or \((c - R, c + R]\), or \((c - R, c + R)\).

Slide 12

End-Point Behavior of Interval of Convergence

When is the interval of convergence a closed interval \([a, b]\), an open interval \((a, b)\), or neither closed nor open \([a, b)\) or \((a, b]\)?

Steps: * a) After obtaining \(c\) & \(R\), compute \(c - R\), and \(c + R\). * b) Substitute the values of \(c - R\) and \(c + R\) instead of \(x\) in the given series, and analyze the resulting series for convergence/divergence. * c) If the series converges for both values, we will get a closed interval. * d) If the series diverges for both values, we will get an open interval.

Slide 13

Radius of Convergence

Suppose that \(L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right|\) exists or is \(\infty\). Then the power series \[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n(x - c)^n\] has radius of convergence \(R = 1/L\). (If \(L = 0\), then \(R = \infty\); if \(L = \infty\), then \(R = 0\).)

Task 1

Use the alternating series test to show that the following series converge.

  • (a) \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{k+1} \frac{1}{k}\)
  • (b) \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{k+1} \frac{k + 3}{k(k + 1)}\)
  • (c) \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{2k + 1}\)
  • (d) \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{k+1} \frac{k}{3^k}\)

Slide 3

Task 1 Solution

(a) Conditions of the alternating series test are satisfied since: \(a_k > 0, \forall k \ge 1\) \[a_k = \frac{1}{k} > \frac{1}{k + 1} = a_{k+1} \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{k \to \infty} a_k = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{1}{k} = 0.\]

(b) Conditions of the alternating series test are satisfied since \(a_k > 0, \forall k \ge 1\) \[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} = \frac{k + 4}{(k + 1)(k + 2)} \cdot \frac{k(k + 1)}{k + 3} = \frac{k^2 + 4k}{k^2 + 5k + 6} < 1 \implies a_k > a_{k+1}\] and \[\lim_{k \to \infty} a_k = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{k + 3}{k(k + 1)} = 0.\]

Slide 4

Task 1 Solution

(c) \(a_k > 0, \forall k \ge 1\) \[a_k = \frac{1}{2k + 1}, \quad a_k = \frac{1}{2k + 1} > \frac{1}{2k + 3} = a_{k+1},\] \[\lim_{k \to \infty} a_k = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{1}{2k + 1} = 0\] All conditions of the alternating series test are satisfied.

(d) \(a_k > 0, \forall k \ge 1\) \[a_k = \frac{k}{3^k}, \quad \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} = \frac{k + 1}{3^{k+1}} \cdot \frac{3^k}{k} = \frac{k + 1}{3k} \le \frac{2}{3} < 1,\] \[\lim_{k \to \infty} a_k = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{k}{3^k} = 0\] All conditions of the alternating series test are satisfied.

Task 2

Classify each series as absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent, or divergent.

  • (a) \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!}\)
  • (b) \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin k}{k^3}\)
  • (c) \(\sum_{k=3}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k \ln k}{k}\)
  • (d) \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k \cos \pi k}{k^2 + 1}\)

Slide 6

Task 2 Solution

(a) \[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!}\] \[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!} \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k!}\]

\[L = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{1}{(k+1)!} \frac{k!}{1} = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{k!}{(k + 1)!} = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{1}{k + 1} = 0\] Since \(L < 1\), the Ratio test implies absolute convergence.

Slide 7

Task 2 Solution

(b) \[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left| \frac{\sin k}{k^3} \right|\] \[\left| \frac{\sin k}{k^3} \right| \le \frac{1}{k^3}\] We know that \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k^3}\) is a convergent p-series with \(p = 3 > 1\). Thus, the original series converges absolutely, by comparison test.

Slide 8

Task 2 Solution

(c) Let’s check for absolute convergence: \(\sum_{k=3}^{\infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^k \ln k}{k} \right| = \sum_{k=3}^{\infty} \frac{\ln k}{k}\) We can use comparison: \(\frac{\ln k}{k} > \frac{1}{k} \, \forall k \ge 3\). Since \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k}\) diverges, \(\sum_{k=3}^{\infty} \frac{\ln k}{k}\) also diverges. Therefore, the series does NOT converge absolutely.

Let’s check for conditional convergence by applying alternating series test, let \(b_k = \frac{\ln k}{k}\). \(b_k > 0 \, \forall k \ge 3\), \[k(k + 1)(b_{k+1} - b_k) = k \ln k + 1 - (k + 1) \ln k = k \ln \frac{k + 1}{k} - \ln k = \ln \left( \frac{k+1}{k} \right)^k - \ln k \xrightarrow{k \to \infty} -\infty\] Thus, \(\exists K_0 \in \mathbb{N} : b_{k+1} - b_k < 0 \, \forall k \ge K_0\). Also, \(\lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{\ln k}{k} = 0\). (Check that \(k < e^{2\sqrt{k}}\), then \(0 < \frac{\ln k}{k} < \frac{2\sqrt{k}}{k} : k \ge 3\)). Thus, the series is conditionally convergent.

Slide 9

Task 2 Solution

(d) \[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k \cos \pi k}{k^2 + 1} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k k}{k^2 + 1}\] Let’s check for absolute convergence: \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^k k}{k^2 + 1} \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k}{k^2 + 1}\). Using limit comparison test: \(\lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{\frac{k}{k^2 + 1}}{\frac{1}{k}} = 1\). Thus the series does NOT converge absolutely.

Let’s check for conditional convergence by applying alternating series test, let \(b_k = \frac{k}{k^2+1}\). \(b_k > 0 \, \forall k \ge 1\), \(\lim_{k \to \infty} b_k = 0\). \[\frac{b_{k+1}}{b_k} = \frac{(k + 1)(k^2 + 1)}{k(k + 1)^2 + k} = 1 - \frac{k^2 + k - 1}{k^3 + 2k^2 + 2k} < 1 \, \forall k \ge 1.\] Thus, the series is conditionally convergent.

Task 3

Find the radius and interval of convergence of the following series. Investigate what happens at the end-points of the region of absolute convergence.

  • (a) \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k!x^k}{k^k}\)
  • (b) \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} kx^k\)
  • (c) \(\sum_{k=2}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{\ln k}\)
  • (d) \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k-1} x^k}{\sqrt{k}}\)

Slide 11

Task 3 Solution

(a) Let \(a_k = \frac{k!}{k^k}\). Apply the Ratio Test: \[\rho = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| |x| = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{\frac{(k+1)!}{(k+1)^{k+1}}}{\frac{k!}{k^k}} \right| |x| = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{(k + 1)!}{(k + 1)^{k+1}} \cdot \frac{k^k}{k!} |x|\] \[= \lim_{k \to \infty} \left( \frac{k}{k + 1} \right)^k |x| = \frac{1}{e} |x|\] The series converges absolutely when \(\rho < 1\), i.e., \(\frac{1}{e} |x| < 1\) or \(|x| < e\). The series diverges when \(\rho > 1\), i.e., \(|x| > e\). Therefore, the radius of convergence is \(R = e\).

  • At \(x = e\): \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k!e^k}{k^k}\). We can prove by induction that \(\frac{k!e^k}{k^k} \ge e \, \forall k \ge 1\). Therefore, our series is divergent by comparison test with \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} e\).
  • At \(x = -e\): \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k!(-e)^k}{k^k} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^k \frac{k!e^k}{k^k}\). Here, it is clear that \(\lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{k!e^k}{k^k} \ge e\) since \(\frac{k!e^k}{k^k} \ge e \, \forall k \ge 1\). Thus, it’s also divergent.

Task 3 Solution

(b) If \(x \neq 0\), then applying the ratio test: \[\rho = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{u_{k+1}}{u_k} \right| = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{(k + 1)x^{k+1}}{kx^k} \right| = |x|\] * The radius of convergence is \(R = 1\) * At \(|x| = 1, |kx^k| = k \to \infty\) diverges * Interval of convergence: \((-1, 1)\) * Absolute convergence occurs in \((-1, 1)\)

Task 3 Solution

(c) Use the Ratio Test: \[L = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{x^{k+1}}{\ln(k + 1)} \cdot \frac{\ln k}{x^k} \right| = |x| \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{\ln k}{\ln(k + 1)}\] Since \(\lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{\ln k}{\ln(k + 1)} = 1\), we have \(L = |x|\). The series converges absolutely when \(L < 1 \implies |x| < 1\). Therefore, the radius of convergence is \(R = 1\).

  • At \(x = 1\): \(\sum_{k=2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\ln k}\). Since \(\ln k < k\) for \(k \ge 2\), we have \(\frac{1}{\ln k} > \frac{1}{k}\). The harmonic series \(\sum \frac{1}{k}\) diverges, so by comparison, \(\sum \frac{1}{\ln k}\) diverges.
  • At \(x = -1\): \(\sum_{k=2}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{\ln k}\). Check the Alternating Series Test conditions:
    1. \(\frac{1}{\ln k} > 0\) for \(k \ge 2\); (2) \(\frac{1}{\ln k}\) is decreasing; (3) \(\lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{1}{\ln k} = 0\). Thus, the series converges conditionally at \(x = -1\) and the interval of convergence is \([-1, 1)\).

Task 3 Solution

(d) Use the Ratio Test: \[L = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{x^{k+1}}{\sqrt{k + 1}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{k}}{x^k} \right| = |x| \lim_{k \to \infty} \sqrt{\frac{k}{k + 1}} = |x|\] The series converges absolutely when \(L < 1 \implies |x| < 1\). Therefore, the radius of convergence is \(R = 1\).

  • At \(x = 1\): \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{k-1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\). Check the Alternating Series Test conditions:
    1. \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} > 0\) for all \(k \ge 1\) (2) \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\) is decreasing for \(k \ge 1\) (3) \(\lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} = 0\). All conditions are satisfied, so the series converges conditionally at \(x = 1\).
  • At \(x = -1\): \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{k-1} \frac{(-1)^k}{\sqrt{k}} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{2k-1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} = -\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\). This is a negative p-series with \(p = \frac{1}{2} < 1\), which diverges. Thus, The interval of convergence is \((-1, 1]\).

Task 4 Solution

(a) For any given \(\varepsilon > 0\), we have to find a suitable \(\delta > 0\), such that \[\left| \sqrt{x - 1} - 2 \right| < \varepsilon \quad \text{whenever} \quad 0 < |x - 5| < \delta\]

For all \(x \neq 5\), we have: \[\left| \sqrt{x - 1} - 2 \right| < \varepsilon \implies -\varepsilon < \sqrt{x - 1} - 2 < \varepsilon \implies 1 + (2 - \varepsilon)^2 < x < 1 + (2 + \varepsilon)^2.\]

Now, let us find \(\delta = \delta(\varepsilon) > 0\) to place the interval \(5 - \delta < x < 5 + \delta\) (centered at \(x = 5\)) inside the interval \((1 + (2 - \varepsilon)^2, 1 + (2 + \varepsilon)^2)\). If we take \(\delta\) to be the distance from \(x = 5\) to the nearer endpoint of \((1 + (2 - \varepsilon)^2, 1 + (2 + \varepsilon)^2)\), that is \[\delta = \min \{4 - (2 - \varepsilon)^2, -4 + (2 + \varepsilon)^2 \} \quad (\text{where } \varepsilon < 4),\] then the inequality \(0 < |x - 5| < \delta\) will automatically place \(x\) between \(1 + (2 - \varepsilon)^2\) and \(1 + (2 + \varepsilon)^2\), and imply that \(\left| \sqrt{x - 1} - 2 \right| < \varepsilon\). This proves that \(\lim_{x \to 5} \sqrt{x - 1} = 2\), for \(\varepsilon < 4\).
