**Slide 3**
**Motivation: What kind of matrices are these?**
**The Big Idea**
Positive definite matrices are the **matrix analogs of positive numbers**.
*   For a number $a \in \mathbb{R}$:
    *   $a > 0$ means $xax > 0$ for any $x \neq 0$.
*   For a symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$:
    *   $A$ is positive definite if $x^T Ax > 0$ for every non-zero vector $x$.

**Why should we care?**
They appear everywhere:
*   **Geometry:** Describe ellipsoids and curvature.
*   **Optimization:** Identify minima of functions.
*   **Statistics:** Covariance matrices.
*   **Engineering:** Stability of systems.

**Slide 4**
**Definition: Positive (Semi-)Definite**
**Definition (Positive Definite Matrix)**
A **symmetric** matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is called <span style="color:red">positive definite</span> (PD) if
$x^T Ax > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n, x \neq 0$.
We denote this as $A \succ 0$.

**Definition (Positive Semi-Definite)**
A symmetric matrix $A$ is <span style="color:red">positive semi-definite</span> (PSD) if
$x^T Ax \geq 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
We denote this as $A \succeq 0$.

**Remark:**
The expression $x^T Ax$ is called a **quadratic form**.

**Slide 5**
**First Examples**
**Example (1 × 1 Matrices)**
A $1 \times 1$ matrix $A = [a]$ is PD if $a > 0$.
*   For $x \neq 0$, $x^T Ax = ax^2 > 0$.
So PD matrices generalize positive numbers.

**Example (Diagonal Matrices)**
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} d_1 & 0 \\ 0 & d_2 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $x^T Ax = d_1x_1^2 + d_2x_2^2$.
*   $A$ is PD if and only if $d_1 > 0$ and $d_2 > 0$.
*   $A$ is PSD if and only if $d_1 \geq 0$ and $d_2 \geq 0$.

**Slide 6**
**Example 2: Diagonal Matrices (2 × 2)**
**Example (Diagonal matrices are easy!)**
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
Take any vector $x = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}$:
$x^T Ax = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = 4x_1^2 + 2x_2^2$

**Is this always positive?**
*   If $x_1 \neq 0$ or $x_2 \neq 0$, then $4x_1^2 + 2x_2^2 > 0$
*   So $A$ is positive definite!

**Slide 7**
**Checking Understanding**
**Example (When is a diagonal matrix NOT positive definite?)**
Consider $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Then $x^T Ax = 4x_1^2 - x_2^2$.
Pick $x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$:
$4 \cdot 0^2 - 1^2 = -1 < 0$
So $A$ is **NOT** positive definite!
<span style="color:red">Rule:</span> A diagonal matrix is positive definite iff **all** diagonal entries are positive.

**Slide 8**
**Example 3: A Non-Diagonal Matrix**
**Example (Simple 2 × 2 matrix)**
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
For $x = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}$:
$x^T Ax = 2x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2$

**How to check if it's positive?** Let's complete the square:
$2x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2 = x_1^2 + (x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + x_2^2) + x_2^2$
$= x_1^2 + (x_1 + x_2)^2 + x_2^2$

**This is a sum of squares!** It's $> 0$ unless $x_1 = 0$, $x_2 = 0$, AND $x_1 + x_2 = 0$, which only happens when $x = 0$.
So $A$ is positive definite!

**Slide 9**
**Eigenvalues: A Magic Shortcut**
**The Eigenvalue Test (Super Simple)**
For a symmetric matrix:
$A$ is positive definite $\iff$ All eigenvalues $\lambda > 0$

**Example (Our previous example)**
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$:
*   Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 1$
*   Both $> 0 \implies A$ is positive definite!

**Slide 10**
**Example (A non-example)**
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$:
*   Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 2$, $\lambda_2 = -1$
*   One eigenvalue is negative $\implies$ NOT positive definite!

**Slide 11**
**The Eigenvalue Test**
**Theorem (Spectral Theorem for PD Matrices)**
Let $A$ be a symmetric matrix. Then:
$A$ is positive definite $\iff$ All eigenvalues of $A$ are $> 0$.
Similarly, $A$ is positive semi-definite iff all eigenvalues $\geq 0$.

**Proof Idea.**
Since $A$ is symmetric, it is orthogonally diagonalizable: $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$, with $Q$ orthogonal. Then
$x^T Ax = x^T Q\Lambda Q^T x = (Q^T x)^T \Lambda (Q^T x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i y_i^2$,
where $y = Q^T x$. This sum is $> 0$ for all $y \neq 0$ iff all $\lambda_i > 0$.

**Slide 12**
**The Cholesky Decomposition**
**Theorem (Cholesky Decomposition)**
If $A$ is symmetric positive definite, then there exists a unique lower triangular matrix $L$ with positive diagonal entries such that
$A = LL^T$.
This is called the **Cholesky decomposition**.

**Example**
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, we have $L = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$:
$LL^T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Slide 13**
**Importance**
*   Numerically stable way to solve $Ax = b$.
*   Analogous to taking a "square root" of a matrix.

**Slide 14**
**Other Equivalent Conditions**
For a symmetric matrix $A$, the following are equivalent:
1.  $A$ is positive definite.
2.  All eigenvalues $\lambda_i(A) > 0$.
3.  All leading principal minors are positive: $\det(A_k) > 0$ for $k = 1, \dots, n$ (Sylvester's criterion).
4.  There exists a matrix $B$ (e.g., $B = L^T$) such that $A = B^T B$ with $B$ invertible.

**Remark**
Sylvester's criterion is a quick test for small matrices. For $2 \times 2$:
$A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}$ is PD iff $a > 0$ and $ac - b^2 > 0$.

**Slide 15**
**Optimization: Finding Maxima and Minima**
**Unconstrained Optimization**
Consider a twice-differentiable function $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$.
*   **Critical point:** $\nabla f(x_0) = 0$.
*   The **Hessian matrix** $H(x_0)$ contains second derivatives: $H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}$.

**Theorem (Second Derivative Test)**
Let $x_0$ be a critical point of $f$.
*   If $H(x_0)$ is **positive definite**, then $x_0$ is a <span style="color:red">local minimum</span>.
*   If $H(x_0)$ is **negative definite**, then $x_0$ is a <span style="color:red">local maximum</span>.
*   If $H(x_0)$ has both positive and negative eigenvalues, $x_0$ is a <span style="color:red">saddle point</span>.

**Slide 16**
**Example: Minimizing a Quadratic Function**
**Example**
Let $f(x, y) = x^2 + 2y^2 + 2xy - 2x + 1$.
1.  Find gradient:
    $\nabla f = \begin{pmatrix} 2x + 2y - 2 \\ 4y + 2x \end{pmatrix}$.
2.  Critical point: solve $\nabla f = 0 \implies x = 2, y = -1$.
3.  Hessian: $H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
4.  Check PD: $a = 2 > 0$, $\det(H) = 2 \cdot 4 - 4 = 4 > 0 \implies$ PD.
5.  Conclusion: $(2, -1)$ is a <span style="color:red">local minimum</span>.

**Slide 17**
**Application 1: Finding Minimum Points**
**Why do we care?**
In calculus, we learn: if $f'(x) = 0$ and $f''(x) > 0$, then $x$ is a minimum.
For functions with multiple variables, the Hessian matrix replaces $f''(x)$!

**Example (Simple 2D function)**
Consider $f(x, y) = x^2 + 2y^2$ (a bowl shape).
*   Critical point at $(0, 0)$
*   Hessian: $H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$
*   Eigenvalues: 2 and 4, both $> 0$
*   So $(0, 0)$ is a <span style="color:red">minimum</span>!

**Slide 18**
**Example (Parabolic dish)**
The shape of a satellite dish: $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$
*   Gradient: $\nabla f = (2x, 2y)$
*   Critical point at $(0, 0)$
*   Hessian: $H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$
*   Eigenvalues: 2 and 2, both $> 0$
*   So $(0, 0)$ is the <span style="color:red">bottom of the dish</span> (minimum)!
This makes sense: the signal is strongest at the center!

**Slide 19**
**Example (Pringles chip shape)**
The function $f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2$ (a saddle).
*   Critical point at $(0, 0)$
*   Hessian: $H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$
*   Eigenvalues: $2$ (positive) and $-2$ (negative)
*   Mixed signs $\implies$ <span style="color:red">saddle point</span> (neither min nor max)

**Slide 20**
**Convexity and Global Minima**
**Definition (Convex Function)**
A function $f$ is **convex** if for all $x, y$ and $t \in [0, 1]$:
$f(tx + (1 - t)y) \leq tf(x) + (1 - t)f(y)$.

**Theorem**
If the Hessian $H(x)$ is positive semi-definite for all $x$, then $f$ is convex. If $H(x)$ is positive definite everywhere, then $f$ is **strictly convex**.

**Consequence**
For a convex function, **any local minimum is a global minimum**. This is crucial in machine learning and engineering optimization.

**Slide 21**
**What is a Quadratic Form?**
**Definition**
A **quadratic form** is a homogeneous polynomial of degree 2 in $n$ variables.

**Example (n = 2 variables)**
$Q(x_1, x_2) = ax_1^2 + 2bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2$
*   $x_1^2$ and $x_2^2$ are square terms
*   $2bx_1x_2$ is the cross term

**Example (n = 3 variables)**
$Q(x_1, x_2, x_3) = a_{11}x_1^2 + a_{22}x_2^2 + a_{33}x_3^2 + 2a_{12}x_1x_2 + 2a_{13}x_1x_3 + 2a_{23}x_2x_3$

**Slide 22**
**Matrix Representation of Quadratic Forms**
**Key Idea**
Any quadratic form can be written as $x^T Ax$ where $A$ is **symmetric**.

**Example (2 × 2 case)**
For $Q(x_1, x_2) = ax_1^2 + 2bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2$:
$x^T Ax = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}$
$= ax_1^2 + bx_1x_2 + bx_2x_1 + cx_2^2$
$= ax_1^2 + 2bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2$

**Slide 23**
**Matrix Representation of Quadratic Forms**
**Remark**
The matrix $A$ is always chosen to be **symmetric** because the off-diagonal entries are averaged: $A_{ij} = A_{ji} = \frac{\text{coefficient of } x_i x_j}{2}$.

**Slide 24**
**Constructing the Symmetric Matrix**
**Rule for Building A**
For a quadratic form $Q(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}x_ix_j$ with $a_{ij}$ not necessarily symmetric:
*   Diagonal entries: $A_{ii} = a_{ii}$
*   Off-diagonal entries: $A_{ij} = A_{ji} = \frac{a_{ij} + a_{ji}}{2}$

**Example**
Write $Q(x, y) = 3x^2 + 4xy + 2y^2$ as $x^T Ax$.
Here $a_{11} = 3$, $a_{22} = 2$, and $a_{12} = 2$ (since $4xy = 2 \times 2 \times xy$).
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$
Check: $x^T Ax = 3x^2 + 2xy + 2yx + 2y^2 = 3x^2 + 4xy + 2y^2$

**Slide 25**
**Why Symmetry Matters**
**Advantages of Symmetric Representation**
1.  Unique representation
2.  Real eigenvalues (Spectral Theorem)
3.  Orthogonal diagonalization
4.  Direct connection to definiteness

**Theorem (Spectral Theorem)**
If $A$ is symmetric, then there exists an orthogonal matrix $Q$ ($Q^T = Q^{-1}$) such that
$A = Q\Lambda Q^T$
where $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ contains the real eigenvalues.

**Slide 26**
**Diagonalizing the Quadratic Form**
**Change of Variables**
Let $y = Q^T x$ (or $x = Qy$). Then:
$x^T Ax = (Qy)^T A(Qy) = y^T (Q^T AQ)y = y^T \Lambda y$
$= \lambda_1y_1^2 + \lambda_2y_2^2 + \dots + \lambda_ny_n^2$

**Example**
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$:
*   Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 3, \lambda_2 = 1$
*   $Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$
*   $x^T Ax = 3y_1^2 + 1y_2^2$

**Slide 27**
**Checking Positivity of Quadratic Forms**
**The Key Question**
Given $Q(x) = x^T Ax$, is $Q(x) > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$?

**Theorem (Eigenvalue Test)**
For a symmetric matrix $A$:
$Q(x) > 0 \; \forall x \neq 0 \iff \text{All eigenvalues } \lambda_i > 0$

**Why?**
After diagonalization: $Q(x) = \lambda_1y_1^2 + \dots + \lambda_ny_n^2$
*   If all $\lambda_i > 0$: sum of positive terms $\implies Q > 0$
*   If any $\lambda_i \leq 0$: choose $y$ with $y_i = 1$, others $0 \implies Q \leq 0$

**Slide 28**
**Example: Checking Positivity**
**Example**
Determine if $Q(x, y) = 2x^2 - 4xy + 5y^2$ is always positive.
**Step 1:** Write in matrix form
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$
**Step 2:** Find eigenvalues
$\det(A - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)(5 - \lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 - 4 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 6)$
**Step 3:** Eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 1 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = 6 > 0$
**Conclusion:** $Q(x, y) > 0$ for all $(x, y) \neq (0, 0)$

**Slide 29**
**Alternative Test: Sylvester's Criterion**
**For a Symmetric Matrix A**
$A$ is positive definite $\iff$ all **leading principal minors** are positive.

**Example (2 × 2 case)**
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}$:
$a > 0$ and $ac - b^2 > 0$

**Example**
For $Q(x, y) = 2x^2 - 4xy + 5y^2$:
*   $a = 2 > 0$
*   $ac - b^2 = 2 \cdot 5 - (-2)^2 = 10 - 4 = 6 > 0$
$\implies$ Positive definite!

**Slide 30**
**Example: Indefinite Quadratic Form**
**Example**
$Q(x, y) = x^2 - y^2$
Matrix form: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$
*   Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 1 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = -1 < 0$
*   $A$ is indefinite
**Sign can change:**
*   $(x, y) = (1, 0): Q = 1 > 0$
*   $(x, y) = (0, 1): Q = -1 < 0$
*   $(x, y) = (1, 1): Q = 0$
<span style="color:red">Not positive definite because we found a vector with $Q < 0$!</span>

**Slide 31**
**Summary: Checking Positivity of Quadratic Forms**
**Three Equivalent Ways to Check if $Q(x) = x^T Ax$ is Positive Definite**
1.  **Definition:** $Q(x) > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$
2.  **Eigenvalue Test:** All eigenvalues of $A$ are $> 0$
3.  **Sylvester's Criterion:** All leading principal minors $> 0$

<span style="color:red">Remember:</span> A quadratic form is positive definite exactly when its symmetric matrix is positive definite!

**Slide 34**
**Quadratic Forms and Their Shapes**
For a real symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, the quadratic form is:
$f(x) = x^T Ax$
**Key Insight:** The definiteness of $A$ determines the geometric shape of $f(x)$.

| Type | Eigenvalues | Shape (2D) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| PD | $\lambda_1 > 0, \lambda_2 > 0$ | Elliptical bowl (paraboloid) |
| PSD | $\lambda_1 > 0, \lambda_2 = 0$ | Parabolic cylinder |
| Indefinite | $\lambda_1 > 0, \lambda_2 < 0$ | Hyperbolic paraboloid (saddle) |

**Slide 42**
**Real SPD vs. Complex HPD**

| Real Case (SPD) | Complex Case (HPD) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Symmetric: $A^T = A$ | Hermitian: $A^* = A$ (conjugate transpose) |
| $x^T Ax > 0$ <br> $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}^n, x \neq 0$ | $x^* Ax > 0$ <br> $\forall x \in \mathbb{C}^n, x \neq 0$ |
| Eigenvalues real and $> 0$ | Eigenvalues real and $> 0$ |

<span style="color:red">Note:</span> $x^* Ax \in \mathbb{R}$ when $A$ is Hermitian.

**Slide 43**
**Definition: Hermitian Positive Definite (HPD)**
A matrix $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ is **HPD** if:
1.  **Hermitian:** $A^* = A$
    $\overline{A_{ji}} = A_{ij} \quad \forall i, j$
2.  **Positive Definite:**
    $x^* Ax > 0 \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{C}^n, x \neq 0$

**Notation:** $A \succ 0$ (in the complex Hermitian sense)

**Slide 44**
**Equivalent Conditions for HPD**
For $A$ Hermitian, the following are equivalent:
1.  $x^* Ax > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$ (definition)
2.  All eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ are **real and positive**
3.  There exists invertible $L$ such that $A = LL^*$ (Cholesky factorization)
4.  All **leading principal minors** are positive (Sylvester's criterion)

**Slide 45**
**Example 1: Real Diagonal Matrix**
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$
*   Hermitian? Yes (real diagonal)
*   Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 3, \lambda_2 = 2 > 0$
*   Quadratic form:
    $x^* Ax = 3|x_1|^2 + 2|x_2|^2 > 0 \quad \forall x \neq 0$

**Slide 46**
**Example 2: Complex Off-Diagonals**
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 - i \\ 1 + i & 4 \end{pmatrix}$
*   Hermitian check:
    $A_{12} = 1 - i, \quad A_{21} = \overline{1 - i} = 1 + i$
*   Eigenvalues: $\lambda \approx 2.76$ and $6.24$ (both positive)
*   Quadratic form:
    $x^* Ax = 5|x_1|^2 + 2 \text{Re}((1 - i)\overline{x_1}x_2) + 4|x_2|^2 > 0$

**Slide 47**
**Example 3: Construction via $A = LL^*$**
Let
$L = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
Then
$A = LL^* = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & i \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2i \\ -2i & 2 \end{pmatrix}$
*   $A$ is automatically HPD because $B$ is invertible *(Note: The slide likely means L is invertible)*
*   $x^* Ax = \|Bx\|^2 > 0$ for $x \neq 0$

**Slide 48**
**Definition: Hermitian Positive Semidefinite (HPSD)**
A matrix $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ is **HPSD** if:
1.  **Hermitian:** $A^* = A$
2.  **Positive Semidefinite:**
    $x^* Ax \geq 0 \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{C}^n$
    (allowing equality for $x \neq 0$)

**Notation:** $A \succeq 0$

**Slide 49**
**Equivalent Conditions for HPSD**
For $A$ Hermitian, the following are equivalent:
1.  $x^* Ax \geq 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$
2.  All eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ are **real and nonnegative** ($\lambda_i \geq 0$)
3.  There exists a (possibly singular) $L$ such that $A = LL^*$ (Cholesky factorization)
4.  All **principal minors** are nonnegative

**Slide 50**
**Example 4: Rank-Deficient HPSD**
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
*   Hermitian? Yes: $A_{12} = i, A_{21} = \overline{i} = -i$
*   Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 2, \lambda_2 = 0$
*   Positive semidefinite (but **not** definite)
*   $x^* Ax = |x_1 + ix_2|^2 \geq 0$

**Slide 51**
**Example 5: Outer Product Construction**
For any vector $v \in \mathbb{C}^n$:
$A = vv^*$
Example with $v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix}$:
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
*   $A$ is HPSD (rank 1, eigenvalues: $\|v\|^2$ and $0$)
*   $x^* Ax = ||v^*x||^2 \geq 0$

**Slide 52**
**Counterexample: Not Hermitian**
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
*   Not Hermitian: $A^* = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix} \neq A$
*   $x^* Ax$ can be complex, positivity not defined
*   Not HPD or HPSD

<span style="color:red">Remember:</span> For complex matrices, **Hermitian** is required before talking about definiteness.

**Slide 53**
**Summary**
*   **Definition:** $x^T Ax > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$.
*   **Key Tests:**
    *   All eigenvalues $> 0$.
    *   All leading principal minors $> 0$ (Sylvester).
    *   Existence of Cholesky factorization $A = LL^T$.
*   **Optimization:** PD Hessian $\implies$ local minimum; convexity.
*   **Applications:** Statistics (covariance), engineering (stability), machine learning (kernel methods).
*   Complex extension of SPD $\to$ **Hermitian Positive Definite**
*   Hermitian condition ensures $x^* Ax \in \mathbb{R}$
*   HPD: $x^* Ax > 0$ for all $x \neq 0 \iff$ eigenvalues $> 0$
*   HPSD: $x^* Ax \geq 0$ for all $x \iff$ eigenvalues $\geq 0$
*   Both factor as $A = LL^*$ (full rank for HPD, possibly singular for HPSD)
