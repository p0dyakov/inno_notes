    Permutation matrices

    Example 1. For given matrix A find its inverse using permutation matrices. \[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Solution. Step 1. Transform the matrix to diagonal form. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{P}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{D}} \] \[ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^{-1} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1} \]

    Slide 3

    Permutation matrices

    Step 2. Transform the matrix to its inverse form. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{D}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}^{-1}}_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}} \]

    \[ \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{A}^{-1} = \left(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1}\right)^{-1} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{A}^{-1} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}^{-1} \]

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}^{-1}}_{\mathbf{A}^{-1}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{P}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{3} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{D}^{-1}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{3} & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}^{-1}} \]

    Gaussian Elimination and LU factorization

    Let us show the equivalence of Gaussian Elimination and matrix factorization. Example 2. \[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Solution. Step 1. Find upper triangular matrix using Gaussian elimination. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} \xrightarrow{r_2 - 2r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}^\star} \xrightarrow{r_3 + 1r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\star\star}} \]

    Slide 5

    Gaussian Elimination and LU factorization

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\star\star}} \xrightarrow{r_3 + 3r_2} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}} \]

    Step 2. Find upper triangular matrix using matrix operators. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{21}} = r_2 - 2r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}^\star} \]

    Gaussian Elimination and LU factorization

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{31}} = r_3 + 1r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}^\star} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\star\star}} \]

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{32}} = r_3 + 3r_2} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\star\star}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}} \]

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{32}} = r_3 + 3r_2} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{31}} = r_3 + 1r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{21}} = r_2 - 2r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}} \]

    Slide 7

    Gaussian Elimination and LU factorization

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{32}} = r_3 + 3r_2} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{31}} = r_3 + 1r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{21}} = r_2 - 2r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}} \] (Matrix product of \(\mathbf{E_{32}}\mathbf{E_{31}}\mathbf{E_{21}}\) is denoted as \(\mathbf{E}\))

    \[ \mathbf{E}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{U} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{U} \]

    \[ \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}_{32}\mathbf{E}_{31}\mathbf{E}_{21} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{E}^{-1} = (\mathbf{E}_{32}\mathbf{E}_{31}\mathbf{E}_{21})^{-1} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{E}^{-1} = \mathbf{E}_{21}^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{31}^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{32}^{-1} \]

    \[ \underbrace{\mathbf{E}_{32}\mathbf{E}_{31}\mathbf{E}_{21}}_{\mathbf{E}} \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{A} = \underbrace{\mathbf{E}_{21}^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{31}^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{32}^{-1}}_{\mathbf{E}^{-1} = \mathbf{L}} \mathbf{U} \]

    Slide 8

    Gaussian Elimination and LU factorization

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{21}^{-1}} = r_2 + 2r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{31}^{-1}} = r_3 - 1r_1} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E_{32}^{-1}} = r_3 - 3r_2}}_{\mathbf{E^{-1} = L}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}} \]

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}} \]

    We have already demonstrated, that LU factorization is equivalent to Gaussian Elimination.

    Forward and backward substitution

    Example 3. Solve given matrix equation using LU decomposition and forward/backward substitution. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}} \]

    Solution. Step 1. Use LU decomposition. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}} \]

    Slide 10

    Forward and backward substitution

    \[ \underbrace{\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{A}} \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{L} \underbrace{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{x}}_{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{b} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{L}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{U}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y} \]

    Step 2. Solve Ly = b for y by forward substitution. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}} \]

    Hence: \(y_1 = \frac{3}{l_{11}} = \frac{3}{1} = 3\) \(y_2 = \frac{3 - 2 \cdot 3}{l_{22}} = -\frac{3}{1} = -3\) \(y_3 = \frac{-2 + 1 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot (-3)}{l_{33}} = -\frac{9}{1} = -8\)

    Slide 11

    Forward and backward substitution

    Step 3. Solve Ux = y for x by backward substitution. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -3 \\ -8 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}} \]

    Hence: \(x_3 = \frac{3}{u_{33}} = \frac{-8}{-4} = 2\) \(x_2 = \frac{-3 + 2 \cdot 2}{u_{22}} = -\frac{1}{1} = -1\) \(x_1 = \frac{3 - 1 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot (-1)}{u_{11}} = \frac{2}{2} = 1\)

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

    LDU decomposition in general

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{U}} \]

    Cholesky decomposition \(\text{LL}^\text{T}\)

    Let us show the specific case for positive definite symmetric matrices. Example 4. \[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ -2 & 8 & 4 \\ -1 & 4 & 11 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Solution. Step 1. Show the procedure of decomposition in symbolic form. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ -2 & 8 & 4 \\ -1 & 4 & 11 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} l_{11} & 0 & 0 \\ l_{21} & l_{22} & 0 \\ l_{31} & l_{32} & l_{33} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} l_{11} & l_{21} & l_{31} \\ 0 & l_{22} & l_{32} \\ 0 & 0 & l_{33} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L^\text{T}}} \]

    Slide 15

    Cholesky decomposition \(\text{LL}^\text{T}\)

    \[ \mathbf{LL}^\text{T} = \begin{bmatrix} l_{11}^2 & l_{11} \cdot l_{21} & l_{11} \cdot l_{31} \\ l_{11} \cdot l_{21} & l_{21}^2 + l_{22}^2 & l_{21} \cdot l_{31} + l_{22} \cdot l_{32} \\ l_{11} \cdot l_{31} & l_{21} \cdot l_{31} + l_{22} \cdot l_{32} & l_{31}^2 + l_{32}^2 + l_{33}^2 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Step 2. Substitute numerical values. \(l_{11}^2 = 1 \Rightarrow l_{11} = \sqrt{1} = 1\) \(l_{11} \cdot l_{21} = -2 \Rightarrow l_{21} = \frac{-2}{l_{11}} = -2\) \(l_{11} \cdot l_{31} = -1 \Rightarrow l_{31} = \frac{-1}{l_{11}} = -1\) \(l_{21}^2 + l_{22}^2 = 8 \Rightarrow l_{22} = \sqrt{8 - l_{21}^2} = \sqrt{8 - (-2)^2} = 2\) \(l_{21} \cdot l_{31} + l_{22} \cdot l_{32} = 4 \Rightarrow l_{32} = \frac{4 - l_{21} \cdot l_{31}}{l_{22}} = 1\) \(l_{31}^2 + l_{32}^2 + l_{33}^2 = 11 \Rightarrow l_{33} = \sqrt{11 - (l_{31}^2 + l_{32}^2)} = 3\)

    Slide 16

    Cholesky decomposition \(\text{LL}^\text{T}\)

    Step 2. Find L and \(\mathbf{L}^\text{T}\). \[ \mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad \mathbf{L}^\text{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

    \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ -2 & 8 & 4 \\ -1 & 4 & 11 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L^\text{T}}} \]

    Slide 17

    Forward and backward substitution

    Solve given equation for positive definite symmetric matrix A. Example 5. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ -2 & 8 & 4 \\ -1 & 4 & 11 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -8 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}} \]

    Solution. Step 1. Use Cholesky decomposition \(\text{LL}^\text{T}\). \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ -2 & 8 & 4 \\ -1 & 4 & 11 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L^\text{T}}} \]

    Slide 18

    Forward and backward substitution

    \[ \underbrace{\mathbf{LL}^\text{T}}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{L}\underbrace{\mathbf{L}^\text{T}\mathbf{x}}_{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{b} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{L}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{L}^\text{T}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y} \]

    Step 2. Solve Ly = b for y by forward substitution. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -8 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}} \]

    Hence: \(y_1 = \frac{3}{l_{11}} = \frac{3}{1} = 3\) \(y_2 = \frac{-8 + 2 \cdot 3}{l_{22}} = -\frac{2}{2} = -1\) \(y_3 = \frac{5 + 1 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot (-1)}{l_{33}} = \frac{9}{3} = 3\)

    Slide 19

    Forward and backward substitution

    Step 3. Solve \(\mathbf{L}^\text{T}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\) for x by backward substitution. \[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{L^\text{T}}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}} \]

    Hence: \(x_3 = \frac{3}{c_{33}} = \frac{3}{3} = 1\) \(x_2 = \frac{-1 - 1 \cdot 1}{c_{22}} = -\frac{2}{2} = -1\) \(x_1 = \frac{3 + 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot (-1)}{c_{11}} = \frac{2}{1} = 2\)

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Existence and uniqueness of LU, LDU and \(\text{LL}^\text{T}\)

    If matrix A is invertible, then it admits an LU (or LDU) factorization if and only if all its leading principal minors are nonzero.

    If a square, invertible matrix A has an LDU factorization (with all diagonal entries of L and U equal to 1), then the factorization is unique.

    In general, any square matrix \(\mathbf{A}^{n \times n}\) could have one of the following: * unique LU factorization, * infinitely many LU factorizations if any of the first \((n - 1)\) columns are linearly dependent, * no LU factorization if the first \((n - 1)\) columns are linearly independent and at least one leading principal minor is zero.

    If simmetric A is positive definite, then the Cholesky decomposition \(\text{LL}^\text{T}\) exists and is unique.

    Appendix

    Positive definite matrix can be defined in different ways. Let us use so called Sylvester’s criterion.

    Real symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{M}^{n \times n}\) is positive definite if and only if all the following matrices have a positive determinant: * the upper left 1-by-1 corner of M, * the upper left 2-by-2 corner of M, * the upper left 3-by-3 corner of M, * … * M itself.

    All of these leading principal minors must be positive for Sylvester’s criterion.
