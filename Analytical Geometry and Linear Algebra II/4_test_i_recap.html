    4.13. Construct a System with a Given Complete Solution (Test I Recap, Problem 1)

    I. Find a \(2 \times 3\) system \(\mathbf{Ax} = \mathbf{b}\) for which the complete solution is \[ x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + w \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

    1. Find a \(3 \times 3\) system with solutions such that when \(\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2 = \mathbf{b}_3\), the system is consistent.
    Click to see the solution

    Key Concept: The complete solution consists of a particular solution plus the null space. We need to reverse-engineer the matrix and right-hand side from this solution.

    Part I: Find a \(2 \times 3\) system

    1. Identify the particular solution and null space direction:

      • Particular solution: \(\mathbf{x}_p = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
      • Null space direction: \(\mathbf{x}_h = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) (free variable \(w\))

    2. Construct the matrix \(A\) such that \(A\mathbf{x}_h = \mathbf{0}\):

      We need the null space to be the span of \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\). This means:

      \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\]

      We can verify: First row: \(1(1) + 0(3) + (-1)(1) = 0\) ✓, Second row: \(0(1) + 1(3) + (-3)(1) = 0\) ✓

    3. Find \(\mathbf{b}\) using the particular solution:

      \[\mathbf{b} = A\mathbf{x}_p = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\]

    4. The system is: \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\]

    Part II: Find a \(3 \times 3\) system with consistency condition

    1. Set up the matrix: Use the rows from Part I and add a third row that is the sum of the first two:

      \[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \\ 1 & 1 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\]

    2. If \(\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2 = \mathbf{b}_3\), then for consistency, the third row must satisfy the same relationship:

      \[\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{b}_1 \\ \mathbf{b}_2 \\ \mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\]

    3. The system is: \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \\ 1 & 1 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\]

    Answer:

    • Part I: \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Part II: \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \\ 1 & 1 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\)
    4.14. Find Complete Solutions of SLAEs (Test I Recap, Problem 2)

    Find the complete solutions of the following systems of linear algebraic equations.

    1. \[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 & 9 \\ -1 & -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \]

    2. \[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 & 4 & 8 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ t \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Click to see the solution

    Key Concept: The complete solution is the sum of a particular solution and the general solution to the homogeneous system.

    Part (a):

    1. Row reduce the augmented matrix:

      \[\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 3 & 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 6 & 9 & 5 \\ -1 & -3 & 3 & 5 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_2 - 2R_1} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 3 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 & 3 \\ -1 & -3 & 3 & 5 \end{array}\right]\]

      \[\xrightarrow{R_3 + R_1} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 3 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 & 6 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_3 - 2R_2} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 3 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]\]

    2. Back substitution:

      • From row 2: \(3z = 3 \Rightarrow z = 1\)
      • From row 1: \(x + 3y + 3(1) = 1 \Rightarrow x + 3y = -2\)
      • With \(y = \alpha\) (free variable): \(x = -2 - 3\alpha\)

    3. Form the complete solution:

      \[\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \alpha \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \alpha \in \mathbb{R}\]

    Part (b):

    1. Row reduce the augmented matrix:

      \[\left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 3 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 6 & 4 & 8 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_2 - 2R_1} \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 3 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right]\]

      \[\xrightarrow{R_3 - R_2} \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 3 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]\]

    2. Continue reducing to RREF:

      \[\xrightarrow{R_1 - \frac{1}{2}R_2} \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 3 & 0 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]\]

    3. Identify free and pivot variables:

      • Pivot variables: \(x, z\)
      • Free variables: \(y = \alpha, t = \beta\)
      • From row 2: \(z + 2t = 1/2 \Rightarrow z = 1/2 - 2\beta\)
      • From row 1: \(x + 3y = 1/2 \Rightarrow x = 1/2 - 3\alpha\)

    4. Form the complete solution:

      \[\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ t \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ 0 \\ 1/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \alpha \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \beta \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad (\alpha, \beta) \in \mathbb{R}^2\]

    Answer:

    • Part (a): \(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \alpha \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Part (b): \(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ t \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ 0 \\ 1/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \alpha \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \beta \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
    4.15. Orthogonal Complement of Row Space (Test I Recap, Problem 3)

    1. Find a basis for the orthogonal complement of the row space of matrix \(A\): \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]

    2. Split \(\mathbf{x} = [3 \; 3 \; 3]^T\) into a row space component and an orthogonal complement component.

    Click to see the solution

    Key Concept: By the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, the orthogonal complement of the row space is the null space. For part (b), we decompose the vector using a full linear combination.

    Part (a):

    1. Recognize the relationship: The orthogonal complement of \(\text{Row}(A)\) is \(\text{Null}(A)\) by the Fundamental Theorem.

    2. Solve \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\):

      \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\]

    3. Row reduce:

      \[\left[\begin{array}{ccc} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 4 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_2 - R_1} \left[\begin{array}{ccc} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right]\]

    4. Read off the solution:

      • \(x + 2z = 0 \Rightarrow x = -2z\)
      • \(y + 2z = 0 \Rightarrow y = -2z\)
      • With \(z = w\) (free variable), the null space is spanned by \(\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\)

    5. Basis for the orthogonal complement:

      \[\text{Null}(A) = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}\]

    Part (b):

    1. Decompose using a full linear combination:

      We need: \(\alpha \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \beta \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \gamma \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\)

      where the first two vectors span \(\text{Row}(A)\) and the third spans its orthogonal complement.

    2. Set up the system:

      \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \\ \gamma \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\]

    3. Solve:

      • From row 3: \(\gamma = 3\)
      • From row 2: \(\alpha + \beta - 2(3) = 3 \Rightarrow \alpha + \beta = 9\)
      • From row 1: \(\alpha - 2(3) = 3 \Rightarrow \alpha = 9\)
      • Therefore: \(\beta = 0\)

    4. Components:

      The decomposition is \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{N}} = [2 \; 2 \; -1]^T\) and \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{R}} = [1 \; 1 \; 4]^T\).

      These sum to \([3 \; 3 \; 3]^T\) ✓. Verification:

      • Is \([1 \; 1 \; 4]^T\) in \(\text{Row}(A)\)? We have rows \([1 \; 0 \; 2]\) and \([1 \; 1 \; 4]\), so yes, it’s the second row.
      • Is \([2 \; 2 \; -1]^T\) orthogonal to both rows? \([1 \; 0 \; 2] \cdot [2 \; 2 \; -1] = 2 + 0 - 2 = 0\) ✓, and \([1 \; 1 \; 4] \cdot [2 \; 2 \; -1] = 2 + 2 - 4 = 0\) ✓.

    Answer:

    • Part (a): A basis for \(\text{Row}(A)^{\perp}\) is \(\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}\) (or any scalar multiple)
    • Part (b): Row space component \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{R}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\), Orthogonal complement component \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{N}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\)
    4.16. Why Are These Orthogonal Statements False? (Test I Recap, Problem 4)

    Why are the following statements false?

    • Statement 1: If \(V\) is orthogonal to \(W\), then the orthogonal complement of \(V\) is orthogonal to the orthogonal complement of \(W\).
    • Statement 2: If \(V\) is orthogonal to \(W\), and \(W\) is orthogonal to \(Z\), then the orthogonal complement of \(V\) is orthogonal to \(Z\).
    Click to see the solution

    Key Concept: These statements claim orthogonality relations that don’t actually hold. We disprove them by finding counterexamples.

    Statement 1 Analysis:

    1. Assume specific subspaces in \(\mathbb{R}^3\):

      \[V = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}, \quad W = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}\]

    2. Verify \(V \perp W\):

      \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \quad \checkmark\]

    3. Compute orthogonal complements:

      \[V^{\perp} = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}, \quad W^{\perp} = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}\]

    4. Test if \(V^{\perp} \perp W^{\perp}\):

      Take \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \in V^{\perp}\) and \(\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \in W^{\perp}\).

      \[\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \neq 0 \quad \text{✗}\]

    5. Conclusion: \(V^{\perp}\) is NOT orthogonal to \(W^{\perp}\). The statement is false.

    Statement 2 Analysis:

    1. Assume specific subspaces in \(\mathbb{R}^3\):

      \[V = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}, \quad W = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}, \quad Z = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}\]

    2. Verify \(V \perp W\):

      \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \quad \checkmark\]

    3. Verify \(W \perp Z\):

      \[\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \quad \checkmark\]

    4. Compute \(V^{\perp}\):

      \[V^{\perp} = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}\]

    5. Test if \(V^{\perp} \perp Z\):

      Take \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \in V^{\perp}\) and \(\mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \in Z\).

      \[\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \neq 0 \quad \text{✗}\]

    6. Conclusion: \(V^{\perp}\) is NOT orthogonal to \(Z\). The statement is false.

    Answer: Both statements are false, as demonstrated by the counterexamples above.

    4.17. Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization (Test I Recap, Problem 5)

    Using the following set of non-orthogonal vectors, determine a corresponding set of orthogonal vectors using the Gram-Schmidt process.

    \[ \mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{c} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Click to see the solution

    Key Concept: The Gram-Schmidt process creates orthogonal vectors by repeatedly subtracting projections. Then we normalize to get orthonormal vectors.

    1. Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization (non-normalized):
      • First vector: \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
      • Second vector: \(\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{b} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}_1}\mathbf{b}\)
        • \(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 = 1(1) + 0(1) + 1(0) = 1\)
        • \(\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 = 1 + 1 + 0 = 2\)
        • \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}_1}\mathbf{b} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ 1/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
        • \(\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ 1/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ -1/2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
      • Third vector: \(\mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{c} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}_1}\mathbf{c} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}_2}\mathbf{c}\)
        • \(\mathbf{c} \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 = 0(1) + 1(1) + 1(0) = 1\)
        • \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}_1}\mathbf{c} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ 1/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
        • \(\mathbf{c} \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 0(1/2) + 1(-1/2) + 1(1) = -1/2 + 1 = 1/2\)
        • \(\mathbf{v}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 1/4 + 1/4 + 1 = 3/2\)
        • \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}_2}\mathbf{c} = \frac{1/2}{3/2}\begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ -1/2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ -1/2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/6 \\ -1/6 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}\)
        • \(\mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ 1/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1/6 \\ -1/6 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/3 \\ 1/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}\)
    2. Normalize to get orthonormal vectors:
      • \(||\mathbf{v}_1|| = \sqrt{1 + 1 + 0} = \sqrt{2}\)
        • \(\mathbf{q}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
      • \(||\mathbf{v}_2|| = \sqrt{1/4 + 1/4 + 1} = \sqrt{3/2} = \sqrt{6}/2\)
        • \(\mathbf{q}_2 = \frac{2}{\sqrt{6}}\begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ -1/2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{6} \\ -1/\sqrt{6} \\ 2/\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}\)
      • \(||\mathbf{v}_3|| = \sqrt{1/9 + 1/9 + 1/9} = \sqrt{3/9} = \sqrt{1/3} = 1/\sqrt{3}\)
        • \(\mathbf{q}_3 = \sqrt{3}\begin{bmatrix} -1/3 \\ 1/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/\sqrt{3} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} \end{bmatrix}\)

    Answer:

    The set of orthonormal vectors is: \[\mathbf{q}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{q}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{6} \\ -1/\sqrt{6} \\ 2/\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{q}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} -1/\sqrt{3} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} \end{bmatrix}\]

    4.18. Solve Using LU-Decomposition (Test I Recap, Problem 6)

    Solve the given matrix equation using LU-decomposition and forward/backward substitution.

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Click to see the solution

    Key Concept: LU-decomposition factors \(A = LU\) where \(L\) is lower triangular and \(U\) is upper triangular. This allows us to solve \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) via two triangular systems: \(L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}\) (forward substitution) and \(U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\) (backward substitution).

    1. Perform LU-decomposition on matrix \(A\):

      Apply Gaussian elimination without row swaps, recording the multipliers:

      \[A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

      • Step 1: Eliminate below pivot in column 1
        • Multiplier for row 2: \(m_{21} = 4/2 = 2\)
        • Multiplier for row 3: \(m_{31} = -2/2 = -1\)
        • After elimination:
        \[\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\]
      • Step 2: Eliminate below pivot in column 2
        • Multiplier for row 3: \(m_{32} = 3/(-1) = -3\)
        • After elimination:
        \[U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\]

    2. Construct the \(L\) matrix from multipliers:

      \[L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

    3. Verify \(A = LU\):

      \[LU = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \checkmark\]

    4. Forward substitution: Solve \(L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}\)

      \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\]

      • \(y_1 = 3\)
      • \(2y_1 + y_2 = 3 \Rightarrow 2(3) + y_2 = 3 \Rightarrow y_2 = -3\)
      • \(-y_1 - 3y_2 + y_3 = -2 \Rightarrow -3 - 3(-3) + y_3 = -2 \Rightarrow -3 + 9 + y_3 = -2 \Rightarrow y_3 = -8\)

    5. Backward substitution: Solve \(U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\)

      \[\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -3 \\ -8 \end{bmatrix}\]

      • \(-4x_3 = -8 \Rightarrow x_3 = 2\)
      • \(-x_2 - 2x_3 = -3 \Rightarrow -x_2 - 2(2) = -3 \Rightarrow -x_2 = 1 \Rightarrow x_2 = -1\)
      • \(2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 3 \Rightarrow 2x_1 - 1 + 2 = 3 \Rightarrow 2x_1 = 2 \Rightarrow x_1 = 1\)

    Answer: \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\)

    4.19. Check Symmetric Positive Definiteness (Test I Recap, Problem 7)

    Check whether the following matrices are symmetric positive definite.

    \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Click to see the solution

    Key Concept: A symmetric matrix is positive definite if and only if all its leading principal minors (determinants of top-left submatrices) are positive.

    Checking Matrix \(A\):

    1. Verify symmetry: \(A^T = A\) ✓
    2. Check leading principal minors:
      • \(D_1 = |2| = 2 > 0\) ✓
      • \(D_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 4 - 1 = 3 > 0\) ✓
      • \(D_3 = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 2\begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} + 1\begin{vmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{vmatrix}\)
        • \(= 2(3) + 1(-2 - 0) = 6 - 2 = 4 > 0\) ✓
    3. Conclusion: All leading minors are positive, so \(A\) is symmetric positive definite.

    Checking Matrix \(B\):

    1. Verify symmetry: \(B^T = B\) ✓
    2. Check leading principal minors:

      • \(D_1 = |5| = 5 > 0\) ✓

      • \(D_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 20 - 4 = 16 > 0\) ✓

      • \(D_3 = \begin{vmatrix} 5 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix}\)

        Expanding along row 1:

        \(D_3 = 5\begin{vmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix} - 2\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix} + 1\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix}\)

        \(= 5(12 - 1) - 2(6 - 1) + 1(2 - 4)\)

        \(= 5(11) - 2(5) + 1(-2) = 55 - 10 - 2 = 43 > 0\) ✓

    3. Conclusion: All leading minors are positive, so \(B\) is symmetric positive definite.

    Checking Matrix \(C\):

    1. Verify symmetry: \(C^T = C\) ✓
    2. Check leading principal minors:

      • \(D_1 = |4| = 4 > 0\) ✓

      • \(D_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{vmatrix} = 12 > 0\) ✓

      • \(D_3 = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{vmatrix}\)

        Expanding along row 2 (easiest):

        \(D_3 = 3\begin{vmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 3(8 - 1) = 3(7) = 21 > 0\) ✓

    3. Conclusion: All leading minors are positive, so \(C\) is symmetric positive definite.

    Answer: All three matrices \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) are symmetric positive definite because they are symmetric and all their leading principal minors are positive.
